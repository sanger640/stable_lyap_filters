    How the monitor and filter work

    The robot imagines many possible futures for its next move, using only its camera latent. If those futures split into different outcomes (a fork), the move is fragile, so the filter changes that same move (slower, a pause, smaller, or backed off) until its futures stop splitting. This is G-CEM, the current filter: no extra policy samples, no hand-made moves. It matched the earlier selection filter in two pre-registered closed-loop tests (Stage 4 + 4b, 600 episodes: 37 vs 39 topples, 492 vs 493 successes) and beat the policy alone (137 → 37 topples). The trigger (sections 1–7) is the same one used by the final filter (\(h_{\text{safe}}=\log_2 3\), 114 → 30 topples in final test 2); figures in section 9 come from that selection filter, and those marked first final test from the earlier \(h_{\text{safe}}=1\) version.

    0The filter in plain words

    Before every move, the robot asks itself: "If my eyes and my arm were a bit worse than usual, would this move still turn out the same way?" If yes, it goes ahead. If the answer is "it could go two very different ways", it picks a nearby move that doesn't have that problem.

    Think of carrying a full cup of coffee past a shelf of glasses. You don't calculate physics; you imagine the next second a few times. If in some versions your elbow clips a glass, you move a little wider or slow down, before anything happens. That is all this filter does, using a learned "imagination" (the world model) instead of a human one.

    1 · LookThe camera image is squeezed into a compact "memory of the scene" (the latent). No object list, no goal.
    2 · ImagineThe world model plays out the policy's next move 64 times, each with slightly different seeing and shaking errors, at normal errors and at 2× worse.
    3 · CheckDo the imagined futures split into two outcomes (a "fork")? Does anything move that the hand isn't touching?
    4 · DecideNo fork: run the policy's move unchanged (about 93% of the time). Fork: search over four simple changes of that same move (speed, pause, shrink, back off along its own reverse) for the smallest change whose futures don't fork; if none passes, hold still.

    What "safe enough" means (the final filter)

    A move must keep a single outcome even if the robot's errors were twice as large. The first version only asked for normal errors, so it often noticed danger at the very moment the block started tipping, too late. Asking for 2× headroom makes it react one step earlier, while there is still a safe alternative.

    What it never uses

    Object positions, the task goal, or examples of failures. Its thresholds come from the robot's own normal behaviour.

    3Imagining futures under uncertainty

    For each future \(j=1,\dots,N\) (\(N=64\)) and uncertainty scale \(s\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} d_j&\sim p_{\text{perc}} &&\text{perception error }(x,y,\psi)\\ \hat z^{\,j}_0&=G(z_0,\;s\,d_j) &&\text{perturbed start latent}\\ \tilde a^{\,j}_t&=a_t-s\,\varepsilon_{j,t} &&\text{measured robot tracking error}\\ \hat z^{\,j}_{t+1}&=f_\theta(\hat z^{\,j}_t,\tilde a^{\,j}_t) &&\text{roll out } a_{1:15}\text{, then hold} \end{aligned} \]

    The endpoints \(e^j_{\text{short}},e^j_{\text{long}}\) are read 1 s and 3 s after the motion. \(p_{\text{perc}}\) and \(\varepsilon\) were measured from the camera pipeline and from real-time controller logs, not tuned.

    4Fork test: do the futures split?

    Project the endpoints onto their main direction, find the best two-way cut, and ask whether two groups explain the futures better than one:

    \[ y_j=u_1^{\top}(e^j-\bar e) \]\[ k^\star=\arg\max_{2\le k\le N-2}\ \tfrac{k(N-k)}{N}\big(\bar y_{\le k}-\bar y_{>k}\big)^2 \]
    \[ \mathrm{Fork}\iff\begin{cases}\mathrm{BIC}_2<\mathrm{BIC}_1 & \text{two groups fit better}\\ D=\dfrac{\sqrt2\,|\mu_a-\mu_b|}{\sqrt{\sigma_a^2+\sigma_b^2}}>2 & \text{clearly separated}\\ \min(n_a,n_b)\ge2 & \text{not one outlier}\end{cases} \]

    \(D\) (Ashman's D) means the two groups are clearly separated, not one wide blob. A fork must appear at both the 1 s and 3 s endpoints.

    5Barrier: how much uncertainty before it forks?

    In plain words

    Before each move the robot asks: "if my perception and motor control were a bit worse than usual, would this move still have one predictable outcome, or could it go two very different ways?" Think of carrying a full cup of coffee. Some motions are fine even if your hand shakes a lot; others spill at the slightest shake. You pick the motion that tolerates the most shake.

    \(h\) is how much shake a move tolerates. We multiply the robot's normal errors by \(s=\tfrac12, 1, 2, 3\) and find the smallest \(s\) at which the imagined futures split (a fork). \(h=\log_2 s\), so each step up means "survives twice the error".

    The rule: a move is allowed if it is at least as robust as standing still (or does not fork even at 2× the robot's normal errors), and does not make things move away from the hand. That is the "barrier": \(h\) is the distance to the edge where outcomes become unpredictable, and the filter never steps closer to that edge than standing still would.

    \(h\)forks atmeaning
    \(-1\)½× errorsvery fragile
    \(0\)1× (normal)fragile
    \(1\)2×marginal: the filter acts
    \(\log_2 3\)3× or neverrobust (= \(h_{\text{safe}}\))
    \(p,\ p^\pi\)a candidate move (next 1.6 s); the policy's own move
    \(s\)error multiplier (1 = the robot's normal errors)
    \(h_{\text{now}}\)margin of "hold still": how fragile the scene already is
    \(h_{\text{safe}}=\log_2 3\)"good enough": no fork even at 2× the robot's errors (the first filter used 1 = normal errors only)
    \(o,\ O_{\max}\)change away from the hand; its normal range for this policy
    \(\gamma=0\)optional slack in the rule (unused)
    \[ h(p)=\log_2\ \min\{\,s\in\mathcal S:\ \mathrm{Fork}(p,s)\,\} \]\[ \mathcal S=\{\tfrac12,\,1,\,2,\,3\}\ \Rightarrow\ h\in\{-1,\,0,\,1,\,\log_2 3\} \]

    (\(h=\log_2 3\) if it never forks.) The idea: a robust move survives even 2–3× the expected errors; a fragile one forks at the errors the robot already has. \(h_{\text{now}}=h(\text{hold still})\) measures how fragile the scene is right now.

    6Regime check: is the world changing away from the hand?

    \[ o(p)=\max_j\ \frac{1}{|\bar M_p|}\sum_{u\in\bar M_p}\tfrac18\big\|e^j_{\text{long}}(u)-z_0(u)\big\|^2 \]

    \(M_p\) is the robot's own region: latent patches within 32 px of the projected gripper path, plus the arm corridor above it. \(\bar M_p\) is everything else. Large \(o\) means the plan moves things the robot is not touching (e.g. a neighbour tipping).

    \[ O_{\max}=\mathrm{P}_{99}\big(o(p^\pi)\big)\ \text{over 300 dev decisions}=0.484 \]

    This is calibrated without labels: it is just the policy's normal range.

    7Safety condition: the rule, written as a control barrier function

    Assume the current regime is safe, and treat the fork as the boundary of the safe set. A plan is admissible if it keeps at least the margin that holding still has (capped at \(h_{\text{safe}}\)), and does not change the world away from the hand:

    \[ \mathrm{Safe}(p)\iff\begin{cases} h(p)\ \ge\ \min\big(h_{\text{now}},\,h_{\text{safe}}\big)-\gamma\\ o(p)\ \le\ O_{\max}\end{cases} \]

    \(h_{\text{safe}}=\log_2 3\) (no fork even at 2× the robot's errors), \(\gamma=0\). The first filter used \(h_{\text{safe}}=1\), which let plans that fork at 2× through; raising the margin makes the filter act one decision earlier (final test 2: 114 → 30 topples). This is the discrete-time CBF condition \(h(x_{k+1})\ge(1-\alpha)h(x_k)\) with \(\alpha=0\), relative to doing nothing; the cap stops it from demanding more than "no fork at 2× error".

    8The filter (G-CEM): the smallest change to the policy's own move

    \[ \theta^\star=\arg\min_{\theta}\ \big\|\,P_{1:5}(G_\theta(p^\pi))-P_{1:5}(p^\pi)\big\|^2+\eta\,\|\Delta^2\cdot\|^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathrm{Safe}\big(G_\theta(p^\pi)\big) \]
    \[ \theta=(v,k,s,b)\in[0,1]\times[0,8]\times[0,1]\times[0,30\,\text{mm}] \]

    It edits the one chunk the policy proposed, with four universal moves: speed \(v\) (re-time the plan slower), pause \(k\) steps before starting, amplitude \(s\) (shrink the move toward where the hand is now), and back-off \(b\) (a ramped retreat along the reverse of the chunk's own net direction). The gripper stays on the policy's clock (re-timing it too, "G2", was tested and rejected in Stage 4b). The cost is how far the executed hand path moves from the policy's, so \(\theta=(1,0,1,0)\), the policy's move unchanged, costs nothing.

    Search and certificate: cross-entropy search (16 candidates × 3 rounds, 32 imagined futures each, the same random draws for every candidate), then the 4 best are re-checked with 64 fresh futures by the world model and a 4-member ensemble; all must pass. Cheapest certified move wins; if none certifies, hold still if holding is safe, otherwise the most robust candidate. It keeps a median 64–67% of the chunk's motion (offline).

    1. Score \(p^\pi\) and "hold still" with 64 futures at each \(s\in\mathcal S\) → \(h(p^\pi),o(p^\pi),h_{\text{now}}\)
    2. \(\mathrm{Safe}(p^\pi)\)? → execute it unchanged. Done.
    3. Else CEM over \(\theta\): sample, score (32 futures), keep the elites, repeat 3×
    4. Certify the 4 best with fresh futures on every model; run the cheapest that passes, else hold
    5. Re-plan from the new image 0.8 s later

    Earlier fixes, kept for the record: selecting among 15 policy samples + 3 hand-made moves (the final-test filter, matched by G-CEM), and free millimetre offsets (F, 6–7% offline risk vs G's 0.7%).

    10How it was evaluated

    • Paired: 600 fresh layouts (episodes 10000–10599), each run with the policy alone, the first filter and the final filter, all three in one cluster job (same seeds, same GPU).
    • Stress setting: normal blocks, 2× measured robot tracking error (about 20% topples alone).
    • Pre-registered: the filter (including \(h_{\text{safe}}=\log_2 3\), chosen on separate dev episodes) and the pass rule were frozen before any test episode ran.
    • Ground truth: for diagnosis, each decision is re-simulated 64 times in the exact simulator.
    • Success (v4 / v4b): no neighbour ever toppled and the red block was extracted (lifted or pulled ≥ 2 cm) without being knocked over first; v4b also requires it not to tip over afterwards.

    11Known limits

    • Remaining failures: 30 of 114 baseline topples still happen (11 of them caused by the filter).
    • More interventions: it acts on 7% of decisions (2× the first filter), slowing the median lift by about 3 s.
    • Which failures it catches: fragile ones, where small errors decide the outcome (Jenga: forks). On SafeLIBERO most of pi0.5's collisions happen in every imagined future (drive-throughs, ~80%), and our port's world model missed the forks that did exist (0 of 13): no safety effect in a 60-episode pilot. New benchmarks are now checked for real forks first (OopsieBench pick_egg: 50–80% of egg-damage moments fork).
    • Speed: G-CEM takes about 26 s per intervention vs 0.8 s per chunk; real time needs a much faster model.
    • Scope: one task in simulation so far. SafeLIBERO did not transfer (above); Physion (monitor) and OopsieBench (filter) are under due diligence; the real robot is pending.

    Which signal warns earliest? (held-out, pre-registered)

    Policy-alone episodes never used for any choice: 60 neighbour topples, 3327 decisions. At the adopted trigger's false-alarm rate (12%), the fork test warns at least 3 chunks ahead for 77% of topples. Measuring how much more the plan spreads the same imagined futures than stopping does warns for 85%. World-model ensemble disagreement, the usual epistemic monitor, manages 38%.

\title{Accurate on Average, Blind at the Fork}

\begin{abstract}
A world model that predicts well on average can still be wrong exactly where it matters for safety:
at forks, where small, realistic deviations send a manipulation task into different outcomes. We
measure this \emph{branching fidelity} with a goal-free fork probe modelled on the human intuitive
physics engine \citep{battaglia2013simulation}: sample the robot's measured execution and perception
error, roll the intended action chunk forward 64 times, and flag the chunk if the futures split into
distinct outcomes. With exact physics and a real camera-based perception model, the probe matches
ground-truth fragility on unseen Jenga layouts ($\kappa$ up to 0.84 against a ceiling of 0.92), also
when outcomes are read from images. Replacing the simulator by a stochastic token-space world model
that predicts futures 3$\times$ better than a no-motion baseline drops agreement to $\kappa$ 0.18: its
sampled spread has the right size but ignores where futures branch. Robots that check actions before
executing them need world models with branching fidelity, and average-error benchmarks do not measure it.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, intuitive physics, runtime safety}

\section{Introduction}
Modern manipulation policies, from diffusion policies to vision-language-action models, act by
committing to short \emph{action chunks} \citep{chi2023diffusion,zhao2023act,black2024pi0}. Before a
chunk runs, a monitor can ask whether it is safe. In contact-rich tasks such as Jenga extraction the
answer hinges on millimetres: the same command can slide the block out or tip a neighbour, depending
on small errors in execution and perception. Such a chunk is precarious even if its nominal outcome is
fine, because uncertainty the robot cannot control decides what happens.

People judge precariousness by running a few noisy mental simulations over an uncertain scene and
checking whether the outcomes disagree, the \emph{intuitive physics engine} (IPE)
\citep{battaglia2013simulation,smith2013sources,sanborn2013reconciling}. A robot can do the same, but it
needs a model to simulate with. Outside simulation that model must be learned, and the debate on
whether robots need world models then becomes concrete: a world model is useful for this check only if
its rollouts \emph{branch where the real dynamics branch}. Prediction error does not measure this.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) A \emph{fork probe} that checks an action chunk before execution without
task labels, failure data or fitted thresholds, by sampling the robot's own measured execution and
perception error (Sec.~\ref{sec:probe}). (2) Validation of the probe with exact physics and real camera
perception on unseen layouts, including reading outcomes from images, which sets the reference a
world model should reach (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). (3) \emph{Branching fidelity} as a world-model
evaluation: a token-space world model with low prediction error still loses the forks, and a
diagnosis shows why (Sec.~\ref{sec:wm}).

\section{Setup and the fork probe}\label{sec:probe}
\paragraph{Task.} A simulated Franka Panda (MuJoCo) extracts blocks from Jenga-like stacks. At every
chunk start a policy commits to an 8-step action chunk, held for 30 further steps. Our chunks come from
recorded extraction episodes, but the probe treats the policy as a black box and sees only the
commanded chunk, one camera image and proprioception, so it applies to any chunked policy.

\paragraph{What counts as unsafe.} A chunk is \emph{fragile} if deviations the robot cannot control,
its execution error and the error in its perception of the scene, can send the outcome into
qualitatively different regimes. This definition needs no goal predicate or failure label, which a
monitor around a generalist policy cannot assume; it isolates cases where the robot's intent no longer
determines the outcome; and in contact-rich manipulation damage typically happens at such boundaries.
A chunk that fails every time is not fragile in this sense and needs a task-level check.

\paragraph{The fork probe.} The probe imagines 64 versions of the chunk. Version $j$ subtracts an
execution-error snippet from the commanded positions, drawn from the robot's measured tracking
residuals (median chunk maximum 3.2\,mm), and starts from a perturbed version of the perceived scene.
The scene is perceived by a frozen DINOv2 encoder \citep{oquab2024dinov2} on one close-camera image
with a keypoint head that regresses the poses of the three blocks in play (2.5\,mm held-out error).
The perception error model is measured once, offline: we run the perception model on held-out
validation images with known poses and fit a zero-mean Gaussian to its errors (a full $9\times9$
covariance over block positions, which captures correlated errors, plus a rotation covariance per
block). Each rollout starts from the estimate plus one draw from this model, as in the IPE. The probe
then reads the 64 endings (object positions, or image tokens) at hold steps 10 and 30 and flags the
chunk if, at both times, the endings projected on their first principal axis prefer two Gaussians to
one by BIC, are well separated (Ashman's $D>2$ \citep{ashman1994detecting}), have at least 2 rollouts in
the smaller group, and keep the same grouping. These are fixed statistical conventions, not fitted to
labels. A variant hedges against an unlucky estimate by drawing 5 scene hypotheses and running the
probe around each (``5 hypotheses'', flag if at least 3 split).

\paragraph{Ground truth.} One real outcome cannot grade a monitor: a fragile chunk may succeed by luck.
Fragility is a property of the spread of possible outcomes, so we compute it with privileged access.
\emph{Fragility truth} runs the probe from the \emph{true} scene in exact physics, with the same
execution and perception error draws: the answer an oracle that knows the scene would give at the
robot's own uncertainty. Two independent oracle runs agree at Cohen's $\kappa$ 0.85--0.92, the ceiling
any monitor can reach. Because this truth uses the same split test, we add a purely physical label:
a \emph{topple fork} is a state where some but not all oracle rollouts (2--62 of 64) knock over a
neighbouring block. Each evaluation uses an unseen test set of stack layouts (up to 60 topple forks
plus 100 safe states), under a protocol frozen before the data existed.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Validating the probe with exact physics}\label{sec:validation}
On 112 development states with exact physics and the true scene, the split test flags all 23 topple
forks and 10\% of the 89 quiet states. With real perception, sampling the perception error is
essential: perception errors of 2--4\,mm are comparable to execution noise, and a probe that trusts
the estimate (``no perception sampling'') reaches only 38--55\% of the ceiling $\kappa$, while the probe
reaches 76--91\% (Table~\ref{tab:panels}). Reading outcomes from DINOv2 tokens of rendered endings
instead of object positions changes few decisions ($\kappa$ 0.86--0.90 between the two), so the probe
works from images alone: on test set C it agrees with fragility truth at $\kappa$ 0.83 and catches 88\%
of topple forks (95\% CI 0.77--0.94) with 7\% false alarms on other states. This is the reference a
world model should reach. Most remaining misses arise when the estimate puts every rollout on the safe
side of the edge (12 of 14 on test set B).

\subsection{Branching fidelity of a learned world model}\label{sec:wm}
So far, rollouts ran in MuJoCo with the true geometry; only the block poses came from the camera.
Outside simulation a learned model must take that role, and the probe becomes its test: given one image
and the 64 perturbed chunks, do its rollouts branch where physics branches? Since outcomes can be read
from image tokens, a world model in token space can be probed exactly like the simulator. Our
candidate encodes the image with DINOv2, compressed by a fixed per-patch PCA to a $28\times28\times8$
latent; the split test on this latent agrees with the position-based test at $\kappa$ 0.93 on held-out
real futures. A flow-matching transition predicts the next latent from the current one and the
action, with fresh noise at every step, so repeated rollouts can differ. It is trained on 8 futures per
image whose start scenes were sampled within the measured perception error, so the spread of its
targets is the robot's own uncertainty. A deterministic version of the same network is the control.
Both predict well on average, beating a no-motion baseline on 19-step latent error by 3--4$\times$. Yet
on test set C the stochastic model reaches $\kappa$ 0.18 and catches 18\% of topple forks, below its
deterministic control ($\kappa$ 0.42) and far below the simulator reference ($\kappa$ 0.83;
Table~\ref{tab:wm}).

\paragraph{Why the stochastic model fails.} Table~\ref{tab:wm} compares, with real futures, the total
spread of the 64 endings, the share of that spread on its main axis, and how much larger it is on fork
states than on others. The stochastic model reproduces the \emph{amount} of spread but not its
\emph{shape} or \emph{placement}: its spread is isotropic and nearly the same on forks and non-forks,
the average uncertainty of futures rather than where they branch; other samplers are worse. The
deterministic model's spread is tiny but aligned with the branch axis and 32$\times$ larger on forks
(Fig.~\ref{fig:forks}b), and started from rendered perturbed scenes it doubles agreement with real
futures ($\kappa$ 0.17$\to$0.37). Per-step noise is not wrong in general (diffusion weather ensembles
stay well dispersed \citep{price2025gencast}); here it was misplaced. As in the IPE, the uncertainty that
matters is in the initial conditions, and roughly right dynamics amplify it along the branch. A second
iteration (a learned operator perturbing the start latent by physical perception error, then
deterministic dynamics) is under evaluation \todo{result}.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Simulation under uncertainty.} Beyond the IPE and counterfactual accounts of physical
support \citep{zhou2023mental}, robotics runs Monte Carlo rollouts over pose, friction or model
parameters to score grasps and pushes \citep{mahler2017dexnet,agboh2018pushing} or to build safety
filters \citep{johansson2025safety,wang2026manidreams}. Closest to our setup, \citet{cannizzaro2024cobra}
sample measured perception and placement noise through a simulator to predict whether a block tower
stands, and \citet{goyal2026stability} perturbs action chunks with policy-scale noise to measure, offline,
how fast deviations grow. These score a task predicate, labels \citep{lerer2016learning} or a growth
rate; \citet{zapolsky2016particle} proposed goal-free detection of divergent particle traces but
evaluated task failure. We judge only whether the outcome set splits, a Monte Carlo test of proximity
to a basin boundary \citep{menck2013basin}.
\paragraph{Runtime monitors.} Latent safety filters use learned failure classifiers, also on Jenga
\citep{nakamura2025generalizing,seo2025uncertainty}; failure detectors use policy uncertainty calibrated
on successes \citep{xu2025failure,agia2024unpacking}. Both measure what a model does not know, not
whether the physics is at a branch.
\paragraph{World models and their uncertainty.} Learned simulators and latent world models
\citep{sanchezgonzalez2020learning,allen2023fignet,zhou2025dinowm,assran2025vjepa2,hafner2025dreamerv3}
struggle with stiff contact \citep{parmar2021fundamental}, and audits find poor physical grounding and
optimism under failure-inducing actions \citep{bear2021physion,motamed2026physicsiq,yang2026mirabench}.
\citet{yu2026evaluated} propose evaluating world models on interventional action branches by per-branch
error; our model has low error yet loses the forks. Stochastic and ensemble models
\citep{chua2018pets,lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} are judged by likelihood or return, not by where their
spread falls.

\section{Conclusion and limitations}
A robot can judge fragility the way people do, by sampling its own perception and execution uncertainty
through a rough simulator. For the question this workshop poses, our results suggest that such
pre-execution checks need a queryable world model with branching fidelity, which the world model we
trained lacks despite low average error, and that in this setting the uncertainty that matters enters
through the initial conditions. Average-error benchmarks would rank this model well; branching fidelity
is a needed additional axis. \paragraph{Limitations.} Simulation, one task family, three tracked blocks;
MuJoCo rollouts use privileged geometry beyond the perceived poses; the perception error model is the
same for every image; fragility truth shares the split test (topple forks are the independent check);
test sets vary in difficulty; single world-model training runs; chunks that fail every time are not
flagged.

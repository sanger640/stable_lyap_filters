\title{Robots Need World Models That Know When the Future Forks:\\
A Goal-Free Probe of Branching Fidelity}
\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Some safety questions are counterfactual: would a slightly different execution of this action, or a
slightly different version of the perceived scene, end in a different regime? Following the human
intuitive physics engine \citep{battaglia2013simulation}, we answer them with a goal-free
\emph{fork probe} that samples the robot's measured execution and perception error, rolls out 64
futures, and alarms when they split into persistent, well-separated groups, with no task labels,
failure data or fitted thresholds. In simulated Jenga extraction with real camera perception, the
probe tracks ground-truth fragility on three fresh panels ($\kappa$ 0.70--0.84 against a ceiling of
0.85--0.92, versus 0.32--0.51 when perception error is ignored), including when outcomes are read
from image tokens. Used as an evaluation of world models, it shows that learned simulators accurate
on average erase forks: a graph-network model halves fork recall, and a stochastic token-space world
model reaches only $\kappa$ 0.18 because its sampled spread is isotropic and ignores where futures
branch. Robots need world models for such questions, but only ones with this \emph{branching
fidelity}, which current models lack and current benchmarks do not measure.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, intuitive physics, runtime safety}

\section{Introduction}
World models are usually judged by average prediction error. For safe manipulation, what often
matters is whether a model knows \emph{where} small, realistic deviations in action or perception
lead to qualitatively different outcomes: the block slides out, or the tower tips. Such a fork is a
narrow, high-consequence boundary, so a model can be accurate on average and still wrong exactly
there. Asking whether a deviation would end in a different regime is a counterfactual query about
futures the robot will not execute; an end-to-end policy does not answer it, a queryable model can.

Humans answer it with an intuitive physics engine (IPE) \citep{battaglia2013simulation}: a few
approximate, noisy simulations over an uncertain scene, whose disagreement signals precariousness;
both perceptual and dynamics noise are needed to explain human judgments
\citep{smith2013sources,sanborn2013reconciling}. We give a robot the same recipe and ask what it
demands of perception and of the world model. \textbf{Contributions:} (1) a goal-free, label-free
\emph{fork probe} with a structure-only alarm; (2) an \emph{uncertainty-aware} probe that samples the
robot's measured perception error, confirmed with real camera perception on three fresh panels and
working fully visually; (3) \emph{branching fidelity} as a world-model evaluation, showing that
learned simulators lose forks, with a diagnosis of why.

\section{The fork probe}
\textbf{Setting and probe.} A Franka Panda extracts a block from a Jenga-like stack in MuJoCo. At
each chunk start the policy commits to an 8-step action chunk, followed by a 30-step hold. The probe
draws 64 execution-error snippets from the robot's measured tracking residuals (median chunk maximum
3.2\,mm), subtracts them from the commanded positions, and rolls out. It alarms if, at hold steps 10
\emph{and} 30, the endings (object positions, or below, image tokens) projected on their first
principal axis prefer two Gaussians to one by BIC, are well separated (Ashman's $D>2$
\citep{ashman1994detecting}), have at least 2 rollouts in the minority, and keep the same partition.
These are generic statistical conventions, not fitted to labels.

\textbf{Uncertainty-aware probe (the IPE).} The robot perceives $\hat s$, not the true scene $s$:
a frozen DINOv2 encoder \citep{oquab2024dinov2} on one close-camera frame plus a keypoint head
regresses the poses of the three blocks in play (held-out error 2.5\,mm). Its validation residuals
define an error model (full $9\times9$ position covariance plus rotation covariances). The
\emph{naive} probe starts all rollouts at $\hat s$; the \emph{aware} probe (v1) starts rollout $j$
at $\hat s+e_j$ with $e_j$ drawn from the error model; the \emph{Bayesian} probe (v2) draws 5
hypotheses $\hat s+d_m$, runs an aware probe around each, and alarms if at least 3 split.

\textbf{Ground truth.} \emph{Fragility truth} applies the aware recipe around the \emph{true} state:
a state is fragile if futures split within the robot's own uncertainty. Two independent seeds (A, B)
agree at $\kappa$ 0.85--0.92, the attainable ceiling. A \emph{harm fork} is a state where 2--62 of
64 truth-A rollouts topple a neighbouring block. Each confirmation uses a \emph{fresh panel} of new
stack layouts (up to 60 topple forks plus 100 safe states) under a protocol frozen before data.

\section{Results}
\textbf{The probe sees physical forks.} With exact physics and the true state, the split test alarms
on all 23 development topple forks at 10\% alarms on quiet states.

\textbf{Sampling the robot's own perception error restores calibrated judgments.} Perception errors
of 2--4\,mm are comparable to execution noise, so the perceived scene is effectively a different
scene and the naive probe misjudges fragility. Aware probes reach 76--91\% of the ceiling, naive
38--55\% (Table~\ref{tab:panels}, Fig.~\ref{fig:forks}c).

\textbf{Reading outcomes from tokens costs little.} The unchanged split test on flattened DINOv2
tokens of rendered endings agrees with the position-based test at $\kappa$ 0.86--0.90 on aware
rollouts; tokens ignore harmless sub-centimetre splits (22 of 24 disagreements on panel 2), acting as
a consequence-scale floor. The fully visual Bayesian probe meets its frozen gate on panel 4 (harm
recall 0.88, 7\% alarms on non-harm states, $\kappa$ 0.83). Misses come from where rollouts start,
not from reading images: on panel 3, token- and position-read v1 each miss 14 of 62 harm forks,
12 of them because the single estimate put the whole rollout cloud on the safe side of the edge.
Panels differ in difficulty, so v2's extra caution is not shown to be better calibrated than v1.

\textbf{Branching fidelity of learned world models.} Replacing the simulator by learned models, with
everything else fixed, loses forks (Table~\ref{tab:wm}). A graph-network state model trained with a
counterfactual objective ranks fragile states well (AUC 0.97), yet under a refined probe keeps 31\%
of topple forks where physics keeps 65.5\%. A stochastic token-space world model (DINOv2 tokens
compressed by a fixed per-patch PCA to $28\times28\times8$, a flow-matching transition trained on 8
futures per image whose starts were sampled within perception error) fails on fresh panel 4
($\kappa$ 0.18, harm recall 0.18), below its own deterministic control ($\kappa$ 0.42).

\textbf{Why the stochastic model fails.} It reproduces the \emph{amount} of spread but not its
\emph{shape} or \emph{placement}: the spread is isotropic and nearly the same on forks and
non-forks, so it learned the average uncertainty of futures, not where they branch; other samplers
are worse. The deterministic model's spread is tiny but aligned with the branch axis and 32$\times$
larger on forks (Fig.~\ref{fig:forks}b); started from rendered perturbed scenes it doubles agreement
with real futures ($\kappa$ 0.17$\to$0.37). As in the IPE, uncertainty should enter through initial
conditions and be amplified by roughly right dynamics, not be added as per-step noise. A second
iteration built on this (a learned operator perturbing the start latent by physical perception
error, then deterministic dynamics trained on full rollouts) is under evaluation \todo{result}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Simulation under uncertainty.} Beyond the IPE and counterfactual accounts of physical
support \citep{zhou2023mental}, robotics runs Monte Carlo rollouts over pose, friction or model
parameters to score grasps and pushes \citep{mahler2017dexnet,agboh2018pushing} or to build safety
filters \citep{johansson2025safety,wang2026manidreams}, always against a task predicate or constraint.
\citet{zapolsky2016particle} proposed detecting divergent behaviour from particle traces but detect
task failure in experiments. Our probe uses the robot's own measured residuals and outcome structure
alone, a Monte Carlo test of proximity to a basin boundary \citep{menck2013basin}.
\textbf{Runtime monitors.} Latent safety filters use learned failure classifiers, including on Jenga
\citep{nakamura2025generalizing,seo2025uncertainty}; failure detectors use policy uncertainty
calibrated on successes \citep{xu2025failure,agia2024unpacking}. These measure what a model does not
know; the fork probe measures whether the physics is at a branch.
\textbf{Physical fidelity of world models.} Learned simulators are accurate on rigid contact
\citep{sanchezgonzalez2020learning,allen2023fignet} and latent world models support planning
\citep{zhou2025dinowm,assran2025vjepa2}, but stiff contact is hard to learn
\citep{parmar2021fundamental} and audits find poor physical grounding and optimism under
failure-inducing actions \citep{motamed2026physicsiq,yang2026mirabench}. We add a complementary axis.

\section{Implications for the debate, limitations and conclusion}
\textbf{Do robots need world models?} For pre-execution safety judgments, a queryable model is needed
by construction, and it must have branching fidelity, which two learned families accurate on average
lack. \textbf{Where should the model live?} Uncertainty belongs in the initial conditions, sampled
from measured perception and execution error; the dynamics can be deterministic and approximate.
\textbf{Can current benchmarks decide?} Not on this axis: average-error and success benchmarks would
rank our failed models well, while the fork probe runs on any model that accepts perturbed actions or
states. \textbf{Limitations.} Simulation only, one task family, three tracked blocks; the MuJoCo
probes use privileged geometry except for the perceived poses; panels vary in difficulty; the split
test's conventions act like fixed thresholds. Still, a robot can judge fragility the way people do,
by sampling its own uncertainty through a rough simulator; learned world models do not yet supply that
simulator. We propose branching fidelity as a standard evaluation axis alongside prediction error and
task success.

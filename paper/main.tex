\title{Robots Need World Models That Know When the Future Forks:\\
A Goal-Free Probe of Branching Fidelity}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Some safety questions are counterfactual by nature: \emph{would a slightly different execution of
this action, or a slightly different version of the scene I perceive, end differently?} A policy
cannot answer this; a model it can query can. People answer it by running a few noisy mental
simulations over an uncertain scene and checking whether the outcomes disagree
\citep{battaglia2013simulation}. We bring this
\emph{intuitive physics engine} to a robot as a goal-free \emph{fork probe}: perturb the intended
action with the robot's own measured execution error and its scene estimate with its measured
perception error, roll out 64 counterfactual futures, and alarm when they split into persistent,
well-separated groups. The probe needs no task label, failure data or fitted threshold. In a
simulated Jenga extraction task with real camera perception (frozen DINOv2), the probe tracks
ground-truth fragility at the robot's own uncertainty scale on three fresh test panels
($\kappa$ 0.70--0.84 against a truth-vs-truth ceiling of 0.85--0.92, versus 0.32--0.51 when
perception error is ignored), and it still works when outcomes are read from image tokens instead of
object states (harm-fork recall 0.88). The same probe then serves as an evaluation axis for world
models, \emph{branching fidelity}. Learned simulators that are accurate on average erase forks: a
graph-network state model halves fork recall, and a stochastic token-space world model trained to
sample futures reaches only $\kappa$ 0.18 because its sampled spread is isotropic and nearly the same
on forks and non-forks. Uncertainty belongs in the initial conditions, not in per-step noise. For the debate on whether
robots need world models, our answer is: for this kind of safety, yes, but only models with
branching fidelity, which today's learned models lack, and benchmarks should measure it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Average prediction error is the standard yardstick for learned dynamics and world models. For safe
manipulation, however, what often matters is whether a model knows \emph{where} small, realistic
deviations in action or perception lead to qualitatively different outcomes: the block slides out,
or the tower tips. A model can be accurate on average and still be wrong exactly there, because a
fork is a low-probability, high-consequence boundary in state space.

This bears directly on whether robots need world models. An end-to-end policy outputs one action;
it does not say whether realistic deviations of that action would end in a different regime. That is
a counterfactual query, and answering it requires a model that can be rolled out under perturbed
actions and perturbed scenes. The question is then which properties such a model must have.

Humans handle this with what \citet{battaglia2013simulation} called an intuitive physics engine
(IPE): a few approximate, noisy simulations over an uncertain scene, whose disagreement signals
precariousness. Both perceptual noise in initial conditions and noise in the dynamics are needed to
explain human judgments \citep{smith2013sources,sanborn2013reconciling}. We ask whether a robot can
use the same recipe at runtime, and what it demands of the robot's perception and world model.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A goal-free, label-free \emph{fork probe}: 64 counterfactual rollouts of
the intended action chunk under measured execution error, with a structure-only alarm (a persistent,
well-separated two-group split). (2) An \emph{uncertainty-aware} version that also samples scenes
within the robot's measured perception error, graded against a ground truth of fragility at the
robot's own uncertainty. With real camera perception it is confirmed on three fresh panels and also
works fully visually. (3) The probe as a world-model evaluation, \emph{branching fidelity}: learned
simulators lose forks, and a diagnosis of why a stochastic token-space world model fails.

\section{The fork probe}
\textbf{Setting.} A Franka Panda extracts a block from a Jenga-like stack in MuJoCo. At each chunk
start the policy commits to an 8-step action chunk, followed by a 30-step hold. The probe answers,
before execution: \emph{do realistic executions of this chunk lead to qualitatively different
futures?}

\textbf{Probe and split test.} We draw 64 execution-error snippets from the robot's measured
tracking residuals (median chunk maximum 3.2\,mm) and subtract them from the commanded positions.
Each rollout yields the object positions (or, below, image tokens) at hold steps 10 and 30. The
alarm fires if, at \emph{both} times, the endings projected on their first principal axis prefer two
Gaussians to one by BIC, are well separated (Ashman's $D>2$ \citep{ashman1994detecting}), have at
least 2 rollouts in the minority, and keep the same partition (at most one rollout switches). Every
constant is a generic statistical convention; none is fitted to labels.

\textbf{Uncertainty-aware probe (the IPE).} A robot does not know the true scene $s$; it perceives
$\hat s$. Perception here is a frozen DINOv2 encoder \citep{oquab2024dinov2} on one close-camera
frame, plus a keypoint head regressing the poses of the three blocks in play (held-out relative
error 2.5\,mm). Its validation residuals define an error model: a full $9\times9$ position
covariance plus per-block rotation covariances. The \emph{naive} probe starts all 64 rollouts at
$\hat s$. The \emph{aware} probe (v1) starts rollout $j$ at $\hat s + e_j$, $e_j$ drawn from the
error model. The \emph{Bayesian} probe (v2) draws 5 hypotheses $\hat s + d_m$, runs an aware probe
around each, and alarms if at least 3 of 5 split.

\textbf{Ground truth.} \emph{Fragility truth} applies the aware recipe around the \emph{true} state
in exact physics: a state is fragile if futures split within the robot's own uncertainty. Running it
with two independent seeds (A, B) gives the attainable ceiling: truth B agrees with truth A at
$\kappa$ 0.85--0.92, not 1, because edge cases flip between draws. A state is a \emph{harm fork}
if 2--62 of the 64 truth-A rollouts topple a neighbouring block ($\geq45^\circ$). Labels are used
only for grading. Each confirmation uses a \emph{fresh panel}: new stack layouts (new reset seeds),
screened with exact probes, up to 60 topple forks plus 100 safe states, under a protocol frozen
before data.

\section{Results}
\textbf{The probe sees physical forks.} With exact physics and the true state, the split test
alarms on all 23 development topple forks at 10\% alarms on quiet states. An earlier, more elaborate
detector with action-space boundary refinement (used for the state-model comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:wm}) reaches 65.5\% of 84 held-out topple forks at 3.5\% quiet alarms.

\textbf{Perception error at the scale of execution noise hides forks; sampling it restores them.}
Perception errors of 2--4\,mm are comparable to execution noise, so the perceived scene is
effectively a different scene and the naive probe misjudges fragility (Table~\ref{tab:panels}).
Sampling the robot's own measured error turns the probe into a calibrated judge: aware probes reach
76--91\% of the truth-vs-truth ceiling, against 38--55\% for naive.

\textbf{Reading outcomes from tokens costs little.} Applied unchanged to the flattened DINOv2 tokens
of rendered endings, the split test agrees with the position-based test at $\kappa$ 0.86--0.90 on
aware rollouts. Tokens ignore sub-centimetre splits that the scale-free position test counts (22 of
24 disagreements on panel 2 were harmless 0.1--6.6\,mm splits), so the camera acts as a natural
consequence-scale floor. The fully visual Bayesian probe meets its frozen gate on panel 4 (harm
recall 0.88, 7\% alarms on non-harm states, $\kappa$ 0.83). Its misses come from where rollouts
start, not from reading images: on panel 3, token-read and position-read v1 miss the same number of
harm forks (14/62). In 12 of them, the 2\,mm error of the single estimate put the whole cloud of
rollouts on the safe side of the edge. Panels differ in difficulty (aware v1 $\kappa$ 0.70 vs 0.84),
so the extra caution of v2 is not shown to be better calibrated than v1.

\textbf{Branching fidelity of learned world models.} We replace the exact simulator by learned
models and keep everything else fixed (Table~\ref{tab:wm}). A graph-network state model trained
with a counterfactual objective ranks fragile states well (AUC 0.97), yet under the same refined
probe it keeps only 31\% of topple forks (8--49\% across 10 seeds) where physics keeps 65.5\%.
Next, a stochastic world model in token space: DINOv2 tokens compressed by a fixed per-patch PCA
($28\times28\times8$, chosen beforehand as the only compression keeping both pose information and
fork structure), with a flow-matching transition trained on 8 futures per image. The futures started
from scenes sampled within perception error, so the target spread is the robot's own uncertainty.
On fresh panel 4 it fails ($\kappa$ 0.18, harm recall 0.18), below its own deterministic control
($\kappa$ 0.42).

\textbf{Why the stochastic model fails.} It reproduces the \emph{amount} of spread (0.98$\times$
real) but not its \emph{shape} or \emph{placement}. The spread is isotropic (15\% on the main axis
versus 57\% for real forks) and nearly the same on forks and non-forks (1.3$\times$ versus
4.8$\times$). It learned the average uncertainty of futures, not where futures branch. Other
samplers (one draw per rollout, half temperature) are worse. The deterministic model's spread is
tiny but structured (69\% on one axis, 32$\times$ larger on forks): its learned dynamics amplify
perturbations along the branch direction. Starting it from rendered perturbed scenes doubles
agreement with real futures ($\kappa$ 0.17$\to$0.37). This matches the IPE: uncertainty should enter
through initial conditions and be amplified by roughly right dynamics, not be added as per-step
generative noise. A second iteration built on this (a learned operator that perturbs the start
latent by physical perception error, followed by deterministic dynamics trained on full rollouts)
is under evaluation \todo{result}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Simulation under uncertainty.} Beyond the IPE \citep{battaglia2013simulation} and
counterfactual accounts of physical support \citep{zhou2023mental}, robotics has long run Monte
Carlo rollouts over pose, friction or model parameters to score grasps and pushes
\citep{mahler2017dexnet,agboh2018pushing} or to wrap planners with safety filters
\citep{johansson2025safety,wang2026manidreams}. These define safety through a task predicate or
constraint. \citet{zapolsky2016particle} proposed detecting divergent robot behaviour from particle
traces, but their experiments detect task failure and leave goal-free detection to future work. Our
probe perturbs the robot's own action with measured residuals and alarms on outcome structure alone,
a Monte Carlo test for proximity to a basin boundary \citep{menck2013basin}.

\textbf{Runtime monitors.} Latent safety filters perform reachability against a learned failure
classifier, including on Jenga extraction \citep{nakamura2025generalizing,seo2025uncertainty}, and
failure detectors use policy-side uncertainty calibrated on successes
\citep{xu2025failure,agia2024unpacking}. These measure what the model does not know. The fork probe
measures whether the physics itself is at a branch, which a confidently wrong model would not flag.

\textbf{Physical fidelity of world models.} Learned simulators reach high accuracy on rigid contact
\citep{sanchezgonzalez2020learning,allen2023fignet}, and latent world models support planning
\citep{zhou2025dinowm,assran2025vjepa2}, yet stiff contact is hard to learn
\citep{parmar2021fundamental}. Recent audits find poor physical grounding and optimism under
failure-inducing actions \citep{motamed2026physicsiq,yang2026mirabench}. We measure a complementary
axis: whether rollouts branch where the physics does.

\section{Implications for the debate}
\textbf{Do robots need world models?} For pre-execution safety judgments of the kind studied here,
a queryable model is required by construction: the question is about futures the robot will not
execute. Our results add a condition. The model must have \emph{branching fidelity}, and two
learned families that are accurate on average do not have it. A model scoring well on average error
can still be useless for this purpose. \textbf{Where should the model live?} Our diagnostics point to
a specific split: uncertainty belongs in the initial conditions, sampled from the robot's measured
perception and execution error, and the dynamics can be deterministic and approximate, as in the
IPE. Generative per-step noise reproduced the right amount of spread in the wrong places.
\textbf{Can current benchmarks decide?} Not on this axis. Average-error and success-rate benchmarks
would rank our failed models well. The fork probe offers a goal-free, label-free test that can be
run on any model that accepts perturbed actions or initial states.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
All results are in simulation, with one task family and three tracked blocks. Our MuJoCo-based
probes use privileged geometry and masses for everything except the perceived block poses. Panels
vary in difficulty, so single-panel comparisons between close monitors are not conclusive. The split
test's statistical conventions are fixed but not label-free in spirit; a reviewer may call them
thresholds. Still, the results support a simple picture: a robot can judge fragility the way people
do, by sampling its own perception and execution uncertainty through a rough simulator. Current
learned world models do not yet supply that simulator, because they do not preserve branching
structure. We propose branching fidelity as a standard evaluation axis for world models used in
manipulation, alongside average prediction error and task success.

\begin{abstract}
World models for manipulation are usually judged by average prediction error or by the return of the
policies that use them, yet picking from a stack,
pushing a tall object or inserting a peg can succeed or fail on a few millimetres of error in the start or
the action. We call a state whose futures, sampled over the robot's own small errors, split into separate
outcomes a \emph{fork}, and propose the \emph{fork probe} to evaluate a world model's \emph{branching
fidelity}: does it branch where physics branches? With a physics engine as the upper reference, the probe
catches 88\% of forks with 9\% false alarms. In an example evaluation, two world models with similar average error, 3--4$\times$ lower than
predicting that nothing moves, differ sharply: a stochastic one catches 18\% of forks, a deterministic one
60\%, and 72\% with uncertain starts. The probe's split test also curates training data: oversampling the
rare branches it finds lifts the deterministic model from 72 to 82\% of forks. Finally, in rigid-body
tasks where one branch is irreversible, a fork marks the edge of the safe set, so the probe doubles as a
label-free safety signal, much as people judge a stack precarious by imagining it fall.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, physical fidelity, evaluation, intuitive physics}

\section{Introduction}
World models are increasingly used to plan, evaluate and safeguard robot policies
\citep{zhou2025dinowm,assran2025vjepa2,hafner2025dreamerv3}, and are judged mainly by average prediction
error, video quality, or the success of the policies that plan with them. Manipulation, however, is full of states where a few millimetres decide the outcome: a block picked
from a stack lifts cleanly or drags a neighbour down, a pushed bottle slides or tips, a peg slides in or
jams, an object nudged near a table edge stays or falls. A world model useful for such tasks
must respond to small changes of state and action as physics does. Average error, dominated by states
where nothing dramatic happens, does not test this, and task success does not show why a model fails. For the question whether robots need world
models, this is a concrete test: a model that cannot reproduce forks cannot be trusted where small errors
matter most.

We call a state whose futures, sampled over the robot's own small errors, split into separate outcomes a
\emph{fork}, and a world model's ability to reproduce forks its \emph{branching fidelity}. We measure it
with a \emph{fork probe} that imagines many futures under sampled errors and tests whether they split.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) The fork probe, an evaluation of branching fidelity to report beside average
error, validated with a physics engine as the upper reference (Sec.~\ref{sec:probe}, \ref{sec:validation}).
(2) An example evaluation: two world models with similar average error differ sharply in branching
fidelity (Sec.~\ref{sec:wm}). (3) Fork-aware training: the probe's split test curates training data and
improves branching fidelity (Sec.~\ref{sec:train}). (4) Forks as a label-free safety signal in rigid-body
manipulation, with its limits (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}).

\section{The fork probe}\label{sec:probe}
\paragraph{Task.} A simulated Franka Panda (MuJoCo) must pick the middle of three upright blocks without
toppling its neighbours (Fig.~\ref{fig:forks}a), a small instance of picking from a stack. A policy commits
to 8-step action chunks \citep{chi2023diffusion,zhao2023act,black2024pi0}; the probe treats it as a black
box and sees only the chunk, one camera image and the robot's joint state.

\paragraph{Forks.} The simulated physics is deterministic, so all uncertainty is the robot's own: its
error in perceiving the block poses (2.5\,mm, measured on held-out images) and in executing commands
(about 3\,mm, measured from tracking). A state is a fork if the outcome splits over $N=64$ futures sampled
from this uncertainty: here, if between 2 and 62 of them topple a neighbour.

\paragraph{Probe.} The probe imagines $N$ versions of the chunk. Each starts from the perceived scene
shifted by a sampled perception error and runs the chunk with a sampled execution error; the robot then
holds still for 30 steps so the outcome settles. The probe flags the chunk if the $N$ endings fall into
two separate groups. We test for separate groups (bimodality), not for large spread: futures can differ
smoothly without a change of outcome (a block sliding a few millimetres further), whereas a fork is a
qualitative split with a gap, and a spread threshold would need tuning per task. The split test uses
fixed statistical conventions, not thresholds fit to labels (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The rollout
engine can be swapped: MuJoCo given the blocks' true shapes, masses and friction, which a real robot does
not know, is the \emph{upper reference}; a learned world model is the system under test.

\paragraph{Ground truth.} One real outcome cannot grade the probe, since a fragile action may succeed by
luck. An oracle runs the $N$ futures from the \emph{true} scene in exact physics and labels forks as above;
two independent oracle runs agree on 99\% of states. Each test set has unseen block layouts, about 60
forks and 100 safe states, and was fixed before testing.

\section{Results}
\subsection{The upper reference: the probe with a physics engine}\label{sec:validation}
Sampling the perception error matters: a probe that samples only execution error catches 65--67\% of
forks, because perception errors are as large as execution errors; adding them raises this to
77--91\% (Table~\ref{tab:panels}). Reading the endings from image features instead of block positions
changes little (77--88\% of forks, 8--12\% false alarms). This is the bar a learned world model should reach.

\subsection{Example evaluation: similar error, different branching fidelity}\label{sec:wm}
A learned model now takes MuJoCo's role. Our models predict in a compact DINOv2 feature space
\citep{oquab2024dinov2} (Appendix~\ref{app:details}); each training image comes with 8 real futures from
perturbed starts, so a model can learn the robot's own uncertainty. We compare a stochastic model (flow
matching), which draws fresh noise at every step, with a deterministic one that predicts the next state
directly. By average error the two look alike: both are 3--4$\times$ better than predicting that nothing
moves, the deterministic one slightly better (0.042 vs 0.055). The fork probe separates them: on test set 3
the stochastic model catches 18\% of forks and the deterministic one 60\% (Table~\ref{tab:wm}).

\paragraph{Where the stochastic model's spread goes.} In physics, futures differ only because starts and
commands differ, and they diverge only near the boundary between outcomes. The stochastic model learns the right \emph{amount} of spread (0.98 of the real futures') but not
\emph{where} it belongs: its futures spread in every direction, almost as much on safe states as on forks
(1.3$\times$ more on forks vs 4.8$\times$ in physics; Fig.~\ref{fig:forks}b). Five further generative variants catch at most
3\% of forks, with the same shapeless spread (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). Its fresh noise at every step
is a likely factor: sharing one noise draw across all futures at test time recovers part of the gap but
not all (Appendix~\ref{app:details}), so the cause deserves further study.

\paragraph{Why the deterministic model comes closer.} Its futures differ only through their perturbed
inputs, so, as in physics, its spread comes from the sensitivity of its dynamics: tiny overall, but along
the branch and 32$\times$ larger on forks. Its weakness is the opposite one: trained with squared error,
it predicts the average of the outcomes it has seen, so near a fork its futures stretch toward both
branches without a gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:forks}b), and rare branches get averaged away.

\subsection{Fork-aware training}\label{sec:train}
The probe's ingredients also serve as a training recipe. \textbf{Uncertain starts:} uncertainty should
enter where it really is, in the robot's knowledge of the scene, not in the physics. A learned operator
perturbs the start by sampled perception error and the deterministic model rolls each start out. Trained
on fork-rich data, this catches 72\% of forks on a fresh test set and plateaus there: longer training, more
data, a frame history or DINO-WM's transformer predictor \citep{zhou2025dinowm} all land at 68--72\%. A
generative model given the same perturbed starts still catches 0\%. \textbf{Data curation with the split test:} without labels, we apply the split test to each
training scene's own real futures, mark the smaller group as the rare branch, and fill half of every batch
with rare-branch futures. On two fresh test sets this lifts the uncertain-start model from 72 to 82\% and
from 82 to 85\% of forks, at 1--4 points more false alarms. Branching fidelity can be trained for, not only measured.

\section{Application: forks as a label-free safety signal}\label{sec:safety}

\vspace{-4pt}

\label{fig:boundary}

The probe mirrors how people judge physical risk. In the \emph{intuitive physics engine} account of
cognitive science \citep{battaglia2013simulation,smith2013sources,sanborn2013reconciling}, people decide
whether a stack will fall by simulating it in their heads a few times, each with slightly different
guesses of where things are, and call it precarious when the outcomes disagree \citep{zhou2023mental}. The fork probe is this check with measured
errors in place of guesses. In rigid-body manipulation one branch is often irreversible: a toppled block
stays toppled, a dropped part stays dropped. A fork then marks a state one small error away from the
unsafe set, i.e.\ the boundary of the safe set (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}), found without failure labels.
Our three-block pick represents such tasks (stacking, picking from clutter). The signal is a proxy, not a failure detector, and does not suit every task: both branches
of a fork may be harmless (a pushed ball rolls left or right), an action that always fails lies inside the
unsafe set and never splits, and gradual harm (spills, deformation) need not split at all. A robot should
pair the flag with a harm signal, e.g.\ whether the outcome can be undone. Our follow-up work builds a label-free
safety filter for manipulation policies on this signal.

\section{Related work}
Robotics runs Monte Carlo rollouts over pose, friction or model parameters to score grasps and pushes
\citep{mahler2017dexnet,agboh2018pushing}, build safety filters \citep{johansson2025safety,wang2026manidreams}
or judge whether blocks stay standing \citep{cannizzaro2024cobra,lerer2016learning};
\citet{goyal2026stability} and \citet{zapolsky2016particle} measure how perturbed rollouts diverge. These
predict a task outcome, a stability label or a divergence rate; our probe is a per-chunk Monte Carlo test
of closeness to a basin boundary \citep{menck2013basin}. Learned world models
\citep{sanchezgonzalez2020learning,allen2023fignet,zhou2025dinowm,assran2025vjepa2,hafner2025dreamerv3}
struggle with stiff contact \citep{parmar2021fundamental} and physical grounding
\citep{bear2021physion,motamed2026physicsiq,yang2026mirabench}; \citet{yu2026evaluated} evaluate branches
created by different actions, ours arise from one action under small errors. Stochastic and ensemble models
\citep{chua2018pets,lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} are judged by likelihood, calibration or return. Safety filters and failure detectors use failure labels, epistemic
or policy uncertainty \citep{nakamura2025generalizing,seo2025uncertainty,xu2025failure,agia2024unpacking},
not whether the physics is at a branch.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
World models for manipulation should branch where physics branches. The fork probe measures this where
average error cannot, and its split test helps train for it; benchmarks should report both. Next:
pretrained world models (V-JEPA 2-AC), other tasks and a real robot.

\section*{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{Scope.} Simulation only, one task with three blocks; other tasks, real images and real
robots are untested, and there is no closed-loop intervention or latency experiment here.
\item \textbf{Ground truth.} The fork label uses the same perception and execution error model that the
probe samples, so it measures agreement with that model, not with real outcomes: if the robot's real errors
are biased or heavier-tailed, real forks lie elsewhere. A real-robot anchor is needed. One Gaussian models perception error for all images, and the engine probe uses the simulator's
true shapes, masses and friction.
\item \textbf{Split test.} It projects the endings on one main axis and fits at most two Gaussians, so it
cannot represent more than two outcome regimes, can miss a split along a minor axis, and cannot see a branch
reached by fewer than 2 of the $N$ futures.
\item \textbf{World models.} Each was trained once, at small scale, from scratch; the DINO-WM comparison
re-implements its predictor on our latents rather than using the released model. The fork-rich training
data of the uncertain-start models was screened with topple counts; only the rare-branch oversampling is
label-free.
\item \textbf{Forks as a safety proxy.} A fork signals danger only when one branch is harmful, as for
toppling rigid bodies. Forks with two harmless branches are false alarms for safety, actions that always
fail are never forks, and gradual harm (deformation, spills) need not split into separate groups.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation details}\label{app:details}
\paragraph{Uncertainty model.} Execution error is drawn from 64 snippets of the robot's measured tracking
residuals (median per-chunk maximum 3.2\,mm). Perception is a frozen DINOv2 encoder on one close-camera
image with a small head that predicts the three blocks' poses (2.5\,mm held-out error); its error model
is a zero-mean Gaussian fitted once, offline, to its errors on held-out images with known poses (a full
$9\times9$ position covariance capturing correlated errors, plus rotation covariances).

\paragraph{Actions and the hold.} An action is an absolute end-effector position target plus a gripper
command, tracked by an impedance controller for 0.1\,s. Chunk step $k$ executes the commanded target plus
the sampled execution error; during the 3\,s hold the arm holds the chunk's last nominal target, without
execution error. The world models receive exactly the same action sequence, with no special hold input.

\paragraph{Split test.} The 64 endings (block positions, or DINOv2 features of the rendered endings) are
projected on their first principal axis at hold steps 10 and 30. The chunk is flagged if, at both times,
two Gaussians fit better than one (BIC), the groups are well separated (Ashman's $D>2$
\citep{ashman1994detecting}) and the smaller group has at least 2 members, and the grouping is the same
at both times. These are fixed statistical conventions, not fitted to labels. On test set 3, every
Ashman's-$D$ threshold from 0.5 to 8 keeps the block-position probe at 88--95\% of forks with 8--10\%
false alarms; the minimum group size matters most (requiring 8 members halves the catch rate).

\paragraph{Ground truth.} The oracle is the probe centred on the true scene: each of its 64 futures starts
from the true block poses shifted by a fresh perception-error draw and uses one execution-error sample (the
same 64 snippets as the probe, different perception seeds). A neighbour topples if it tilts more than
45$^\circ$ by the end.

\paragraph{World models.} The latent compresses DINOv2 patch tokens by a fixed per-patch PCA to
$28\times28\times8$; applied to real futures, the split test on this latent still catches 97\% of forks.
The dynamics are a convolutional network with six residual blocks conditioned on the action. One model
step covers two simulator actions (0.2\,s), so the 8-step chunk plus the 30-step hold is 19 model steps;
the quoted prediction error is the open-loop latent error at the end of this horizon (0.055 stochastic,
0.042 deterministic, 0.176 for predicting that nothing moves, i.e.\ copying the current latent). The stochastic model is a flow-matching transition
that draws fresh noise at every step; the deterministic one predicts the next latent directly. Each
training image comes with 8 real futures whose starts were perturbed by sampled perception error.

\paragraph{Generative variants.} Whole-clip generation, an energy-score loss, 4$\times$ more fork data,
and an action-sensitivity loss. On 160 held-out states from the training domain (75 forks) they catch
0--3\% of forks at 0--1\% false alarms, with spread 1.1--1.6$\times$ that of the real futures and only
7--10\% of it on the main axis.

\paragraph{Noise placement.} With the same trained stochastic network on 60 held-out training-domain states
(35 forks), changing only its test-time noise: fresh noise at every step catches 6\% of forks; one noise
draw shared by all futures, 26\% from perturbed starts; the deterministic model, 51\%.

\paragraph{Uncertain starts.} A learned operator perturbs the start latent by a sampled perception error
and the deterministic network rolls each perturbed start out. On the 160 training-domain states it
catches 52\% of forks at 7\% false alarms, and 69\% at 8\% with 4$\times$ more fork-rich data; there,
the perturbations were paired with the real futures' own draws. On the fresh test sets every draw is
independent, as in deployment. Variants with 3$\times$ longer training, more fork data, a 3-frame history
or DINO-WM's transformer predictor \citep{zhou2025dinowm} reach 68--72\% on the training-domain states.

\paragraph{Rare-branch oversampling.} For every training state, the split test is applied to the latents
of its real futures; if it splits, the smaller group at hold step 30 is the rare branch. Each batch draws
half its futures from rare branches and half from the other branch of the same starts. Model and loss are
unchanged.
